Doses per vial: planning a peptide protocol before you order

The question people ask after the first vial runs out sooner than expected is almost always the same one: how many doses was that supposed to be? It is worth answering before ordering rather than after, because the answer decides how many vials a titration schedule needs and how much diluent each one should get.

The only formula involved

doses per vial = vial contents (mg) / dose per injection (mg)

That is the whole thing. Note what is absent: the volume of bacteriostatic water. Adding more water does not create more peptide. It spreads the same amount over a longer plunger travel, which changes how many units you draw, never how many doses exist. If a concentration change appears to change the dose count, something in the arithmetic is wrong.

Turning doses into weeks

On a once-weekly protocol, doses per vial is weeks per vial directly. A 10 mg vial at 0.5 mg weekly is 20 weeks on paper. In practice it is not, and the reason is the second constraint most planning ignores.

The in-use window usually beats the dose count. A reconstituted vial has a limited shelf life once it has been pierced, typically far shorter than 20 weeks. So the real number of doses a vial delivers is the smaller of two figures: the arithmetic dose count, and the number of doses that fit inside the in-use window. If the arithmetic says 20 doses and the window allows 4 weekly injections, that vial is a 4-dose vial and the rest is waste. That is the single most common source of a peptide budget being wrong.

Choosing reconstitution volume around the plan

Once you know the dose, pick the diluent volume so the draw lands in a readable range on a U-100 syringe, roughly 5 to 50 units. Concentration is mg in vial / mL added and the draw is dose / concentration x 100 units.

  • 10 mg vial, 1 mL water, 0.5 mg dose: 10 mg/mL, draw 5 units. Readable but tight.
  • 10 mg vial, 2 mL water, 0.5 mg dose: 5 mg/mL, draw 10 units. Comfortable.
  • 10 mg vial, 4 mL water, 0.5 mg dose: 2.5 mg/mL, draw 20 units. Very easy to read, but check the vial physically holds 4 mL first.

The vial's own capacity is the ceiling here, and it is smaller than people assume. A standard 2 mL vial will not take 4 mL no matter what the arithmetic prefers.

Planning a titration, not a single dose

Titration schedules step the dose up, so the vial count is not one number. Work it stepwise: for each step, doses at that dose level divided into vial size, then round up to whole vials because a partly used vial at one dose level rarely carries cleanly into the next. Doing that in a spreadsheet works. Doing it in something that also holds the actual injection log works better, which is why I moved mine into the Peptide Tracker & Calculator instead of keeping the plan and the record in two different places.

What to write on the vial

Three things, at the moment of reconstitution: the date, the resulting mg/mL, and the units per dose for whatever dose you are currently on. If the dose changes, update the third one. A vial labelled only with the compound name is a vial you will eventually have to discard because you cannot reconstruct what is in it.
